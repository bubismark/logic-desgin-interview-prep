CBUSY: pipelining a 12×12 multiply under a timing budget

A real datapath block needed to multiply two 12-bit numbers every cycle. A plain a * b didn't meet timing. This page builds up, from the gate level, why addition, subtraction, multiplication and division each cost the logic levels they do, how bit widths grow, and the general recipe for pipelining any of them — worked through on this exact 12×12 case.

The problem

One stage of this datapath needed to compute a * b every cycle, where a and b are each 12-bit unsigned values — a 24-bit product out of a 12-bit×12-bit input. Written down it's one line of RTL and it's functionally correct the moment you simulate it. The problem isn't correctness, it's timing: at the target clock frequency, a single combinational a * b is too deep — the longest chain of logic between the input register and the output register is longer than one clock period allows. Static timing analysis reports negative slack on exactly that path, and no amount of re-simulating fixes it, because the issue isn't logical, it's physical: too many gate delays in series before the next clock edge arrives.

This wall has nothing to do with this specific project — it's a direct consequence of general facts about how deep addition, subtraction, multiplication and division each get as bit-width grows. This page works through all four, bottom-up from individual gates, so that for any N-bit arithmetic problem and a target logic-level budget, you know before writing a line of RTL how many pipeline stages you'll need and roughly how to shape them.

Logic levels, critical path, and where a "budget" comes from

Between any two flip-flops sits some amount of pure combinational logic — gates with no clock edge in between. Each gate costs some propagation delay. The critical path is the slowest (deepest) chain of gates between any launching flop and any capturing flop, and it alone decides how fast you're allowed to clock the design:

T_clk  ≥  t_clk-to-Q  +  (levels × t_per_level)  +  t_setup  +  margin

Rather than tracking picoseconds, it's useful to reason in logic levels: the count of gates/cells stacked in series on the critical path. If your library's rough delay per level is t_per_level, rearranging gives a logic-level budget:

X  =  (T_clk  -  t_clk-to-Q  -  t_setup  -  margin)  /  t_per_level

That's "max logic level of X": the number of gate delays you can legally stack in series between two registers at your target frequency. Any function whose natural depth exceeds X will not meet timing — this isn't an RTL-quality problem, it's physics. The only two escapes are (a) find a shallower logic structure for the same function, or (b) put a register in the middle of it, i.e. pipeline it into shallower pieces.

Adders: from gates to a budget

Inside a full adder — why it costs 2 logic levels

A single-bit full adder takes three inputs (a, b, and the carry-in cin from the bit below it) and produces two outputs: sum and cout. The cleanest way to see why it costs exactly 2 logic levels is to build it from two intermediate signals that every adder design uses — generate and propagate:

  • g = a · b — "this bit position generates a carry on its own," regardless of any incoming carry.
  • p = a ⊕ b — "this bit position would propagate an incoming carry straight through."

Both g and p come directly from the two primary inputs a and b — a single AND gate and a single XOR gate, computed in parallel, ready after 1 logic level. Everything else is built from g, p and cin:

  • sum = p ⊕ cin — one more XOR gate, combining the level-1 result with the incoming carry → ready at level 2.
  • cout = g + (p · cin) — "a carry comes out either because this bit generated one, or because it propagated one that arrived." Real standard-cell libraries implement this whole expression as one compound AND-OR cell (an AOI/OAI-style gate is a standard, physically-available library cell, not two separate gates) — so it's also ready at level 2, the same time as sum.

Chaining N of them: ripple-carry depth ≈ 2N

Bit i's cin is exactly bit i−1's cout. So in a plain ripple-carry adder, the carry has to ripple all the way from bit 0 to bit N−1 before the top bit's sum is valid — each bit repeats the same 2-level cost, chained in series.

Bit growth: adding N bits to M bits

If the two operands aren't the same width, the narrower one is conceptually zero-extended up to the wider one's width before adding — its missing high bits are just 0. The result always needs one more bit than the wider operand, to hold a possible carry-out of the very top bit:

Faster adders: trading gates for depth

You can beat the 2N ripple figure by restructuring how the carries are computed, without changing the function at all:

  • Carry-lookahead (CLA): compute every bit's g and p in parallel (level 1, same as before — none of them depend on a neighbor). Then, instead of rippling carries bit by bit, combine g/p pairs across growing spans — bit0&1 combine into a 2-bit group, then that group combines with the 2&3 group into a 4-bit group, and so on — exactly the same binary-tree idea used later on this page to reduce a multiplier's partial products. That takes O(log N) levels instead of O(N).
  • Carry-select: compute a block's sum twice in parallel — once assuming carry-in 0, once assuming carry-in 1 — then mux in the right answer once the real carry-in shows up. Trades extra adders (and area) for shorter depth.
  • Carry-skip: a middle ground — group bits into blocks; if an entire block would simply propagate an incoming carry straight through, skip the ripple through that block with one extra "skip" gate.

Concretely, for just 4 bits: ripple-carry costs ≈2×4=8 levels; a lookahead/prefix structure costs ≈1 level (generate g,p) + 2 combine levels (log₂4=2) = 3 levels total. The gap widens fast as N grows, since ripple scales linearly and lookahead scales logarithmically.

carry-lookahead adder vs. ripple-carry adder — logic levels

None of this removes the ceiling, it just raises it: O(log N) still grows, and the extra gates/fan-out/power aren't free. For a wide enough or power-constrained enough datapath, you eventually pipeline regardless — which is exactly where this page is headed.

Subtraction: addition with a twist

Hardware essentially never builds a separate subtractor circuit. Two's-complement arithmetic makes A − B identical to A + (~B) + 1: invert every bit of B, and feed the carry-in of the very first bit with a 1 instead of a 0. That's the whole trick.

Cost: one extra level, same bit-growth rule

The XOR-with-sub stage sits in front of the adder's own first level, so it costs one additional logic level in series: an N-bit adder/subtractor costs ≈2N+1 logic levels instead of 2N — subtraction is, for all practical purposes, the same price as addition. Bit growth follows the identical rule as addition: subtracting an M-bit number from an N-bit number needs max(N,M)+1 bits to represent the full signed result safely (the extra bit becomes the sign bit rather than a carry-out, but it's the same "one guard bit" story).

Multipliers: from rows to a budget

Partial products: N bits times M bits

A multiply is fundamentally a sum of shifted partial products. For an N-bit operand A times an M-bit operand B, you generate M rows — one per bit of B — each row being A itself, AND-masked by that one bit of B and shifted left by that bit's position. All M rows then have to be added together to get the full N+M-bit product. (Picking the shorter operand to be the one you expand into rows keeps the row count — and therefore the reduction tree below — as shallow as possible.)

Reducing the rows: ripple vs. tree

Option A — ripple them in one at a time

Chain the rows through a sequence of adds, one after another (what a synthesizer builds by default for a plain a * b if left unconstrained — structurally this is an "array multiplier"). The critical path runs through roughly M additions back to back. Multiplication's natural depth isn't "one add's worth," it compounds across every row.

Option B — reduce with a binary tree

Pair the rows up and add them in parallel (row0+row1, row2+row3, …), halving the row count; repeat. This takes ceil(log₂ M) levels instead of M — much shallower. Each level is still a real add with its own ~2×width cost, and the operand widths grow at every level you climb.

Even with the tree trick, total depth is roughly (tree levels) × (per-level add cost, which keeps growing) — nowhere near "1 level," and it routinely blows a realistic per-cycle budget even for widths that "sound small," like 12 bits. That's the precise answer to why a 12×12 multiply is hard on timing: you're not paying for one add, you're paying for log₂M adds in a row, each one wider than the last.

Bit growth: N bits times M bits

Other techniques: cutting the row count itself

Everything above assumes one row per bit of B. Booth encoding (and its common radix-4 variant, "modified Booth") looks at pairs of bits of B at once and produces one signed partial-product row per pair instead of per bit — roughly half the rows, which directly means one fewer tree level. It's the multiplier-world analogue of carry-lookahead for adders: same function, restructured to trade a bit of extra per-row control logic for a shallower reduction tree.

Division: why it's a different animal

Addition, subtraction and multiplication all have one thing in common: every bit of work can be figured out immediately from the inputs, so independent pieces can run in parallel and be combined with a tree. Division breaks that assumption.

Shift-subtract-restore: one iteration

Long division by hand is the model: at each step, you try to fit the divisor into the current remainder, and you only know whether it fits by actually trying it. In hardware (restoring division), one iteration is: shift the running remainder left and bring in the next dividend bit, try trial = remainder − divisor, look at the sign of the result, and either keep it (quotient bit = 1) or throw it away and restore the old remainder (quotient bit = 0).

Why it's a chain, not a tree

Here's the key structural difference from multiplication: iteration i needs the actual remainder that iteration i−1 produced — there's no way to know what to subtract until you've seen the previous step's result. Multiplication's partial-product rows are all independent of each other the instant the inputs arrive, which is exactly what makes a tree possible. Division's iterations are not independent — they're a straight data-dependency chain, full stop.

Bit growth: quotient and remainder

For an N-bit dividend divided by an M-bit divisor: the quotient needs at most N bits (worst case, divisor=1, quotient=dividend). The remainder always needs at most M bits, because the division algorithm's own invariant guarantees remainder < divisor at every step.

Truncating vs. flooring quotients

The algorithm above naturally produces truncating division (rounds toward zero) when you divide magnitudes and reapply signs afterward — this is what most hardware integer dividers do by default. Flooring division (rounds toward negative infinity) needs one more conditional correction step: if the mathematical quotient is negative and the remainder came out non-zero, decrement the quotient by 1 and add the divisor back into the remainder.

quotientremainderextra hardware
truncating (−7 ÷ 2)−3−1none — the natural output

Non-restoring division

Non-restoring division removes the separate "restore" step: instead of trial-subtracting and conditionally undoing it, each iteration does one add or one subtract, chosen by the sign of the previous iteration's remainder (not this one), with a single small correction only at the very end if the final remainder is negative. The per-iteration depth is still ≈2M (one add-or-subtract, selected the same control-XOR way as the adder/subtractor combo above), but the restore-then-proceed dependency is gone — a real, if modest, simplification of the chain, not a change in its fundamentally sequential nature.

Breaking any of this into a pipeline: the general recipe

Strip away which operation it is, and the question is always the same shape: I have a combinational function of total depth D(N,M), and a budget of X logic levels per stage — where do I cut it? There are four general strategies, and which one applies depends on whether the function's pieces are independent of each other or chained.

1. Flat split

For a function that's a single uniform chain — like a ripple-carry adder's bit-by-bit carry propagation — cut it into ceil(D/X) roughly equal pieces and register between them. Each stage passes its result (and the still-pending carry) forward to the next.

2. Tree split

For a function built from independent parallel sub-problems — like a multiplier's partial products — compute them all in parallel (stage 1), then reduce hierarchically with a register between each level. Gives log-depth stage count instead of linear.

3. Chunk + recombine

Break the N/M-bit operands themselves into smaller chunks small enough to solve in one cheap stage, then combine the chunk results over subsequent stages. This is the general form behind the worked 12×12 example below, and it has a direct analogue in division: resolving more than 1 quotient bit per iteration (higher-radix / SRT division) is exactly "chunk the iterations to cut their count," the same trade as picking bigger multiplier chunks.

4. Sequential reuse

For a chained, data-dependent function — division above all — you often can't tree-ify it. Either spatially unroll into N pipeline stages (full throughput, N× the area), or reuse one small stage over N clock cycles (1× the area, 1/N the throughput). This area-vs-throughput knob is the default choice for dividers precisely because division is usually rarer and more latency-tolerant than multiplication in a design.

Worked micro-example: flat-splitting a wide adder

Say you need a 32-bit add and your budget is X=16 logic levels per stage. A plain ripple adder costs ≈2×32=64 levels — 4 stages' worth. Max single-stage ripple width at this budget is X/2=8 bits, so split the 32 bits into four 8-bit groups, each its own pipeline stage, with the carry-out of one group registered and fed in as the carry-in of the next group's stage one cycle later.

The 12×12 multiplier below is the chunk+recombine strategy; a sequential divider reusing one iteration block over many cycles is strategy 4, already diagrammed above. Worked example: 12×12 under budget X, stage by stage

Split operand A into 2 chunks of 6 bits, and operand B into 4 chunks of 3 bits — the largest chunk sizes whose single combinational multiply still comfortably clears budget X. That's a chunk+recombine pipeline: 2 × 4 = 8 partial products generated in parallel, then reduced.

Flattened row-major (A0's four products, then A1's four), those 8 values reduce through a 3-level binary tree — ceil(log₂8) = 3 levels — pairing neighbors first:

Why 9 → 12 → 18 → 24, exactly: shift-then-add

This isn't the ordinary "add two N-bit numbers, get N+1 bits" rule — that rule assumes both operands sit at the same bit position, and here they don't. Look at the actual stage-2 combine: sum2[0] <= pp[0] + (pp[1] << CHUNK_B). pp[0] is a 9-bit value at bits [8:0]. pp[1] is also 9 bits, but it represents a chunk with weight 2³ (it's B1, bits [5:3] of the original operand), so it has to be shifted left by 3 before it means anything — once shifted, it occupies bits [11:3]. You're not adding two same-position numbers; you're merging two overlapping fields that together span bits [11:0] — 12 positions, not 10.

A distinction worth being precise about: pp[1] itself never becomes a 12-bit register. It stays exactly 9 bits — a shift doesn't change what the number is, only where its wires get connected next. "Shift left by 3" means: wire 0 of pp[1] connects to wire 3 of whatever it's feeding, wire 1 to wire 4, and so on — wiring, not extra flip-flops. The 12-bit width only exists at the input of the adder that receives both operands: pp[0] zero-extended with 3 extra 0s on top, and pp[1] zero-extended with 3 extra 0s on the bottom (because it's been slid up by the shift). Neither original 9-bit register grew — the 12-bit-ness is a property of the adder stage 2 builds, not of pp[0] or pp[1] individually.

Separately: does adding them inside that 12-bit span ever carry out to a 13th bit? That needs the real numbers, not just positions — max pp[0] = 63×7 = 441, max pp[1] = 441 too, shifted: 441×8 = 3528. Max possible sum = 441 + 3528 = 3969. A 12-bit field holds up to 4095. 3969 ≤ 4095, so no — it fits with room to spare, and that's specifically because 441 is well under the 511 a 9-bit field could theoretically hold; that slack is what absorbs the overlap without carrying out.

The general rule: when you add a value to a shifted version of another value — exactly what happens every time partial products (or any place-value terms) get combined — the plain "two N-bit numbers need N+1 bits" rule doesn't apply directly. Work out which bit positions each term touches after its shift, take the union, and that span is your baseline width. Then separately check the real achievable maximum sum against that span to see if one more carry bit is needed on top.

The same reasoning repeats, verified, at every later stage on this page — right through to the final width matching the fundamental N+M=24 rule from the multiply section above:

Logic levels per stage, and why

Stage 1 isn't "free" just because it's cheap enough for one cycle — it's itself a small multiply, built from the exact same partial-product-and-reduce recipe, just small enough to clear budget X in one hop. Working it out precisely (not approximately): each 6×3 cell generates 3 rows (one per bit of the 3-bit operand) via AND gates (1 level), then reduces those 3 rows with 2 sequential adds (3 rows can't be perfectly paired, so it's a short chain, not a tree) — first an 8-bit add (max value 63×3=189, so 16 levels), then a 9-bit add on top of that (max value 63×7=441, so 18 levels). Total: 1 + 16 + 18 = 35 logic levels for stage 1 — and that number has to be ≤ X for this chunk size to be valid at all.

Comparing chunk-size choices: is (6,3) actually a good pick?

The chunk sizes (6 bits for A, 3 bits for B) weren't the only option. A and B could each be split any number of ways, as long as the chunk size divides 12 evenly: 12×1, 6×2, 4×3, 3×4, 2×6, 1×12 — and A and B don't have to use the same chunk size as each other. Here's the general method for judging any choice, and what it reveals about this one.

The general method

1

Depth of one small multiply

For chunk sizes (a,b), each individual a×b partial product costs its own internal depth — computed by the exact same generate-then-tree-reduce recipe as the whole multiply, just at a smaller scale (this is the same recursive idea used to get stage 1's 35 levels above).

2

How many partial products

(12/a) × (12/b) of them, each with a known max value (2a-1)(2b-1) and a known shift i×a + j×b.

3

Reduce via a tree, pairing neighbors

Same shift-then-add rule from above, applied repeatedly: at each level, pair the closest-shifted survivors, compute the real max value of the combination, derive the width from that, and the logic levels from 2×width.

4

Two different totals matter

No-pipeline total = sum of every stage's depth (what you'd need in one un-pipelined shot). Pipelined worst stage = the single deepest stage (what actually limits the clock, once every stage gets its own register).

What the comparison actually shows

  • There's a real trade-off curve, not one obvious winner. Tiny chunks (2,2) make each individual partial product almost free (9 levels) but explode the tree out to 36 pieces, giving the worst no-pipeline total of the set (179). Big chunks (6,6) make the tree tiny (just 3 stages) but make each individual partial product expensive (61 — deeper than this design's entire final stage).
  • For a pipelined design, almost none of this changes the achievable clock speed. Look at the "pipelined worst stage" column: it's 48 for nearly every split tested, because the very last combine always has to produce the full 24-bit answer no matter how you got there, and a 24-bit add is always ≈48 levels regardless of how the earlier stages were chunked. Chunking earlier stages doesn't make the clock faster — only (6,6) changes that number at all, and it changes it for the worse (61), because it pushed too much work into one ungated stage.
  • So what does chunking actually buy you, if not clock speed? Fewer total pipeline stages (less latency) and less area (smaller adders doing less wasted work on structurally-zero bits). Among the splits that hit the standard 48-level ceiling, (6,3) has the fewest stages (4) and the best no-pipeline total (143) of anything tested — a genuinely good pick, not an arbitrary one.

Latency vs. throughput, once it's pipelined

Splitting the multiply into 4 registered stages costs you 4 cycles of latency — the first result doesn't appear until the input has worked its way through every stage. But once the pipeline is full, a brand-new pair of 12-bit operands can enter on every single cycle, because every stage is now shallow enough to finish within one clock period. Latency and throughput are different numbers, and a deep pipeline is a perfectly good trade as long as something downstream can tolerate the delay.

Input a enters cycle 1 and its product comes out cycle 4 — 4 cycles of latency. But b, c, d… keep entering every cycle right behind it, with one result emerging every cycle from cycle 4 onward. Full throughput, paid for with pipeline depth instead of stalling.

A generic implementation

Fresh names, not tied to any real codebase — written to match the exact numbers worked out above (9→12→18→24 bits, 4 stages) so you can check the diagrams against real RTL line by line. Comments mark the magic numbers and the questions an interviewer is likely to ask about each one.

// Generic re-implementation of the pipelined multiplier worked through
// above. 12x12 unsigned multiply, 4 pipeline stages, fully pipelined
// (accepts a new a,b pair every single cycle; out_valid tracks when
// each product comes out the other end).

module pipelined_mult_12x12 (
    input  logic        clk,
    input  logic        rst_n,        // active-low, synchronous

    input  logic        in_valid,     // a,b are valid this cycle
    input  logic [11:0] a,
    input  logic [11:0] b,

    output logic        out_valid,    // product is valid this cycle
    output logic [23:0] product
);

    // Chunk sizes: the largest single-cycle multiply that clears the
    // assumed timing budget (see "Worked example" above for the ~31
    // logic-level derivation of why 6 and 3, not something bigger).
    // MAGIC NUMBERS -- re-derive these, don't just scale them, if
    // WIDTH_A / WIDTH_B ever change.
    localparam int CHUNK_A = 6;
    localparam int CHUNK_B = 3;

    // Shadows in_valid through all 4 stages so out_valid lands on the
    // exact cycle `product` becomes meaningful.
    // INTERVIEWER Q: why a 4-bit shift register instead of a counter?
    //   -> this pipeline can have 4 DIFFERENT transactions in flight at
    //      once, each needing its own valid bit -- a counter can't track that.
    logic [3:0] valid_sr;

    // ---------------- Stage 1: generate ----------------
    // 8 independent 6b x 3b multiplies, fully parallel.
    // INTERVIEWER Q: why 9 bits here, not some "rounder" 12?
    //   -> max value is 63*7=441, and 441 needs exactly 9 bits (2^9=512).
    //      Size every product from its real max value, never from a
    //      convenient-looking number.
    logic [8:0] pp [8];

    always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
        if (!rst_n) begin
            for (int i = 0; i < 8; i++) pp[i] <= '0;
        end else begin
            pp[0] <= a[5:0]  * b[2:0];   // A0 x B0 -> shift  0
            pp[1] <= a[5:0]  * b[5:3];   // A0 x B1 -> shift  3
            pp[2] <= a[5:0]  * b[8:6];   // A0 x B2 -> shift  6
            pp[3] <= a[5:0]  * b[11:9];  // A0 x B3 -> shift  9
            pp[4] <= a[11:6] * b[2:0];   // A1 x B0 -> shift  6
            pp[5] <= a[11:6] * b[5:3];   // A1 x B1 -> shift  9
            pp[6] <= a[11:6] * b[8:6];   // A1 x B2 -> shift 12
            pp[7] <= a[11:6] * b[11:9];  // A1 x B3 -> shift 15
        end
    end

    // ---------------- Stage 2: reduce 8 -> 4 ----------------
    // Pair NEIGHBORS (adjacent shift amounts) -- the "pair neighbors
    // first" rule above. Each pair's shift amounts are always exactly
    // CHUNK_B (3) apart, so every add here stays exactly 12 bits.
    // INTERVIEWER Q: what breaks if you paired pp[0] with pp[7] instead?
    //   -> their shifts are 15 apart, forcing a much wider (and deeper)
    //      adder here for zero benefit -- pairing order is not cosmetic.
    logic [11:0] sum2 [4];

    always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
        if (!rst_n) begin
            for (int i = 0; i < 4; i++) sum2[i] <= '0;
        end else begin
            sum2[0] <= {3'b0, pp[0]} + ({3'b0, pp[1]} << CHUNK_B);
            sum2[1] <= {3'b0, pp[2]} + ({3'b0, pp[3]} << CHUNK_B);
            sum2[2] <= {3'b0, pp[4]} + ({3'b0, pp[5]} << CHUNK_B);
            sum2[3] <= {3'b0, pp[6]} + ({3'b0, pp[7]} << CHUNK_B);
        end
    end

    // ---------------- Stage 3: reduce 4 -> 2 ----------------
    // sum2[0]/sum2[1] and sum2[2]/sum2[3] sit CHUNK_A (6) bits apart in
    // absolute position -- same shift-by-a-fixed-constant pattern as above.
    logic [17:0] sum3 [2];

    always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
        if (!rst_n) begin
            for (int i = 0; i < 2; i++) sum3[i] <= '0;
        end else begin
            sum3[0] <= {6'b0, sum2[0]} + ({6'b0, sum2[1]} << CHUNK_A);
            sum3[1] <= {6'b0, sum2[2]} + ({6'b0, sum2[3]} << CHUNK_A);
        end
    end

    // ---------------- Stage 4: final reduce 2 -> 1 ----------------
    // The widest, deepest add in the whole pipeline (~48 logic levels as
    // a plain ripple adder -- see "Worked example" above). First
    // candidate to split further if your real budget X is tight.
    always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
        if (!rst_n) product <= '0;
        else        product <= {6'b0, sum3[0]} + ({6'b0, sum3[1]} << CHUNK_A);
    end

    always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
        if (!rst_n) valid_sr <= '0;
        else        valid_sr <= {valid_sr[2:0], in_valid};
    end

    assign out_valid = valid_sr[3];

    // INTERVIEWER Q: is this truly fully pipelined -- can in_valid be 1
    // on every single cycle with zero stalls?
    //   -> yes: every stage is an unconditional always_ff with no
    //      backpressure signal anywhere in this module, so a new a,b
    //      pair is accepted every cycle and a new product comes out
    //      every cycle once the pipeline has filled.

endmodule

Cheat sheet

Adders

  • Full adder: g=a·b, p=a⊕b at level 1 (from primary inputs); sum=p⊕cin and cout=g+(p·cin) at level 2 (one compound gate for cout). 2 logic levels per bit.
  • Ripple-carry N-bit add: ≈2N logic levels (linear in width).
  • Max single-cycle ripple-add width at budget X: N_max ≈ X/2 bits.
  • Adding N bits + M bits → result width max(N,M)+1 bits (the carry-out guard bit); depth stays ≈2×max(N,M), not 2×(N+M).
  • Faster structures (carry-lookahead/select/skip): ≈O(log N) depth, more gates/fan-out. Raises the ceiling, doesn't remove it.

Subtraction

  • A − B = A + (~B) + 1: XOR every B bit with a sub control, feed sub as the adder's initial carry-in.
  • Cost: ≈2N+1 logic levels — one extra level over a plain adder, for the control-XOR stage.
  • Bit growth: same as addition, max(N,M)+1 bits, extra bit becomes the sign bit.

Multipliers

  • N-bit × M-bit → M partial-product rows (pick the shorter operand to expand), each N bits, shifted; reduce with ripple (≈M adds in series) or a binary tree (ceil(log₂M) levels, each level's add cost ≈2×(growing width)).
  • Bit growth: N bits × M bits → exactly N+M bits, no overflow possible beyond that.
  • Chunking both operands into a,b-bit pieces → (N/a)(M/b) partial products, each a cheap a×b multiply; fewer, fatter rows → fewer tree levels, at the cost of each chunk's own internal depth (which must itself stay ≤ X).
  • Pair neighboring shift offsets first at every tree level to keep intermediate adder widths (and depth) minimal for as long as possible.
  • Booth encoding: halves the row count by encoding 2 bits of one operand per row instead of 1.

Division

  • One restoring-division iteration: subtract (≈2M levels, M=divisor width) + sign test + restore mux ≈ 2M+1 logic levels per quotient bit.
  • Iterations are a data-dependency chain, not independent — no tree reduction is possible, unlike multiplication.
  • Unrolled into one combinational block: ≈N×(2M+1) levels — essentially never done. Either spatially pipeline into N stages (full throughput, N× area) or reuse one stage over N cycles (1× area, 1/N throughput).
  • Quotient width ≤ N bits; remainder width ≤ M bits (remainder < divisor, always).
  • Truncating (toward zero) is the natural hardware output; flooring (toward −∞) needs one extra correction add+mux when the quotient is negative and the remainder is non-zero.
  • Non-restoring division: replaces trial-subtract+conditional-restore with a single add-or-subtract chosen by the previous iteration's sign, plus one final correction — same ≈2M per-iteration depth, simpler control, still fundamentally a chain.

Pipelining, in general

  1. Fix budget X from your target clock and library delay-per-level.
  2. Classify the function: is it a chain (ripple adder, division iterations) or does it decompose into independent parallel pieces (multiplier partial products)?
  3. Chain → flat split into ceil(D/X) equal pieces, carrying state (carry, running remainder) across each register boundary — or, if the dependency is too tight to pipeline usefully, fall back to sequential reuse of one stage over many cycles (1× area, 1/N throughput).
  4. Independent pieces → tree split: generate all pieces in parallel (stage 1), reduce ceil(log₂(count)) levels, one register per level.
  5. Either way, you can also chunk the operands first (smaller sub-problems, each cheap enough for one stage) before recombining — this is the lever that cuts total stage count, at the cost of raising each chunk's own internal depth toward the budget.
  6. Re-check every stage's actual depth against X once widths have grown (bit-growth rules above); split further wherever one doesn't fit — usually the last, widest stage.
  7. Total pipeline depth = stage count from the steps above. That's latency in cycles; throughput is 1 result/cycle for a true pipeline (flat or tree split with every stage filled), or 1 result per N cycles for sequential reuse.

Takeaways

  • "Logic levels" = gate delays stacked in series between two flops; your clock period directly caps how many you're allowed, which is exactly what a timing budget X means.
  • A full adder is 2 logic levels because generate/propagate let both outputs be computed from a 1-level head start, with the second level's carry logic packed into one compound library cell. Subtraction reuses the same hardware for one more level of control.
  • Addition's cost scales linearly with bit width (~2 levels/bit, ripple-carry); multiplication's cost is worse, because it's fundamentally a sum of many shifted rows — even a smart binary-tree reduction still pays log₂M real adds in series, each wider than the last.
  • Division is structurally different again: its steps are a true data-dependency chain, not independent pieces, so it can never be tree-reduced the way a multiplier can — it's either fully unrolled into a pipeline or run sequentially over many cycles, and the latter is the common default.
  • The fix for any arithmetic block too deep for one cycle is always one of the same few moves: chunk it into pieces small enough to fit the budget, then either tree-reduce (independent pieces) or chain-pipeline / reuse sequentially (dependent pieces), registering at each boundary.
  • The widest, deepest stage is always the last one in a reduction tree. That's usually where you end up needing either a faster adder architecture or one more pipeline split — budget for it rather than being surprised by it.
  • Pipelining trades latency for throughput, not correctness for speed. A 4-stage pipeline is 4 cycles slower to first answer and no slower at all in steady state.
